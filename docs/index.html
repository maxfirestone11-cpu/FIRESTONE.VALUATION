How the grades and values work

Who’s graded. Offensive and defensive players with at least 100 snaps (kickers, punters and fullbacks left out). Early in the season the bar is 20 snaps per week played. The snap slider only changes what you see; grades always compare a player with everyone at his position who qualifies.

Grade, 50 to 95. Position-specific stats, each scored against the position average, weighted, then ranked: the best player at a position gets 95 and the lowest 50. Quarterbacks: EPA per dropback, completion % over expected, TD-minus-INT rate, sack rate, rushing EPA. Running backs: EPA and yards per carry, scrimmage yards and first downs per snap, fumbles. Receivers and tight ends: receiving yards per snap, EPA per target, target share, catch rate, first downs. Edge and interior linemen: sacks plus half of QB hits per 100 snaps, tackles for loss. Linebackers: tackles, TFL, pass rush, plays on the ball. Corners and safeties: passes defended, interceptions and forced fumbles per 100 snaps, tackling.

Offensive line. Public data doesn’t record who allowed a sack, so each lineman is graded on three things that are his own. Pass protection: the pressures and sacks by the defenders lined up across from him that week (from both teams’ depth charts), split by how much of the game he played, and compared with others at his exact spot, since tackles face more rushers than centers. Run blocking: EPA on runs through his gap, and how much more often those runs succeed than his team’s other runs. Penalties he personally committed. It’s still an estimate: stunts, blitzes and help from a tight end can send a pressure to the wrong man.

Projected worth. The grade’s percentile is placed on the current veteran market at that position (the top contracts by yearly average, rookie deals left out), then adjusted for age: full value through each position’s peak (running back 25; linebacker, corner and receiver 27; edge, interior line, safety and tight end 28; offensive line 29; quarterback 32), a small bump under 24, then a yearly decline.

Surplus is projected worth minus average yearly pay. Cost per snap is yearly pay divided by the player’s snap pace over 17 games. Trend is the change in grade over the last two weeks.

Limits. A few weeks is a small sample, so a backup with two great games can grade like a star. Box scores miss coverage, blocking and pressures that charting services track. Use this to find names worth a closer look.

Data. nflverse: snap counts from Pro Football Reference, play-by-play stats and EPA, player IDs, and contracts from OverTheCap. Rebuilt automatically every Tuesday.
